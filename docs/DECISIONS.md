## #786 — Regular pay counts the lower level after one change of pay, never more than a usual month of the new pay (2026-10-05)

**Context.** #785's owner replay on the shipped code (2026-10-04): the one biweekly payroll was found, but the household read `pay-changed` — the Social Security wage-base step (net pay rises once the year's pay passes the cap, falls back in January) sat inside the last eight paychecks. Under "one level" alone, a household with that step stays on the three-month median for about half of every year. It was recorded as kept-as-shipped because treating a settled step as a new level would also annualize a run of overtime (critic cycle 6, P1-1), and the rows cannot tell the two apart.

**Decision (owner's choice, verbatim option, 2026-10-05):** "Pay steps — Regular pay keeps working through one step in pay: a Social Security wage-base step-up, a raise, or a cut. Today any step within the last 8 paychecks drops the plan back to the 3-month median, which is what's happening on your own account right now. The fix plans on the LOWER of the two pay levels, so it never overstates income and a run of overtime can't inflate it." Not chosen this time (still open): the bonus line (#784's open half), the first-charge fix (C.5 residual), two-paycheck households.

**Rule.** Condition 3 of `regularPayFromRows` also passes when the in-band paychecks among the last eight paydays split, in date order, into an earlier and a later run that are each within 2% and do not overlap (every paycheck of the higher run above every paycheck of the lower), and the LOWER run and the LATER run each hold at least two paychecks. The paycheck is then the lower run's median (rounded), never more than the newest paycheck; where several splits qualify, the lowest wins. For a WEEKLY or BIWEEKLY reading the monthly figure is then the lesser of that paycheck at its yearly rate and a usual month (× 4 / × 2) of the smallest paycheck after any qualifying split. The newest-payday guards (the newest payday an ordinary paycheck; no payroll deposit after it) and conditions 1, 2, 4 and 5 are unchanged. `RegularPayStream.step` (`{ direction, newestCents, sinceCents }`, null on one level) carries the change to the one sentence author (glass box, both Ask answers), which says the pay rose or fell, names the newest paycheck, shows the lesser-of arithmetic, and states the widened rule in the code's own terms; the row label reads "Income (regular pay after a pay change, monthly)" and the /budgets note "your regular pay since a change in pay, counted low (the arithmetic is on Guilt-free)".

**Why the lower level answers cycle 6.** A rise is a raise, overtime, or a withholding that stops for the rest of the year; the earlier level is right or low for each. A fall is the newer pay whichever it was. Overtime that started AND stopped inside the eight is two changes, and keeps the median — the cycle-6 shape stays locked.

**Why the usual-month cap (critic cycle 2, P1-A).** A change can also be a new job under the same payroll name with a different rhythm: twice a month (24 a year) after every two weeks (26), or four fixed dates a month (48) after weekly (52). The dates cannot rule it out — a maker brute force over every alignment fits six twice-a-month paydays to a 14-day grid (February's 14-day gaps) and eight 1st/8th/15th/22nd paydays to a 7-day grid — so the rhythm stays "biweekly"/"weekly" while the new pay comes 24/48 times. Capping at a usual month of the new pay is exactly what such a job brings, so the figure cannot exceed it in any reading. It costs a true every-two-weeks household nothing on a rise above 26 ÷ 24 − 1 = 8.33% (the usual net Social Security step for a high earner), the excess on a smaller rise, and 1/13 after a fall (two paychecks a month, not 26 ÷ 12) until eight paychecks agree. Twice-a-month and monthly readings already count the low way and are not capped.

**Why two paychecks on each side and no overlap.** One paycheck is not a level (a short check, a transition amount), and one new payday cannot show its rhythm (cycle 3, P2-1); runs that touch or overlap are drift, not a step. Each keeps the median for that pay period. Each side must itself be within 2%, so varying overtime above a steady base, and varying hourly pay (which #785 keeps on the median), are not pulled onto their minimum.

**Effects, stated (worked in `tests/edge-cases/regular-pay-plans-the-month.md`).** Over a wage-base year with a +10% net step: the median for the first raised paycheck's period; base pay at 26 ÷ 12 from the second; the median for one pay period when a single base paycheck is left among the eight; the raised level once all eight show it (the existing one-level rule — it holds until January); the median for one period after the January step down; two base paychecks a month from the second January paycheck until the eight agree. A raise of more than 2% reaches the figure only once all eight paychecks show it — low, never high. Where the rule hands back to the median (seven of eight risen, one fallen, a rise and fall inside the eight) the median can sit ABOVE the lower level — no worse than before this slice, and the owner's call whether to close it (cycle 1, P2-2; STATUS).

**Alternatives.** Keep as shipped (owner chose the change). Plan on the newer level once it settles (annualizes overtime — cycle 6). The lowest level after any number of changes, or the smallest of the eight (pulls varying pay onto its minimum). Detect the Social Security step itself (the rows carry net pay only; neither the gross nor the cap is known). For P1-A: wait for more paychecks after the change to confirm the rhythm (rejected — six twice-a-month paydays fit the old grid, so the wait would void most of the feature); count every changed stream 24 a year (rejected — about the median, no gain); read job changes from raw descriptors (rejected — real payroll descriptors carry varying trace numbers, and two employers on one processor can normalize alike).

**Critic (Opus, separate contexts).** Cycle 1 **FAIL — 0 P0 / 1 P1 / 2 P2 / 3 P3**; a generative old-vs-new run over 47,872 households found 0 one-level or non-`pay-changed` households changed, 0 figures above the newest paycheck.
